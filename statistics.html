Error Analysis
& Statistics

Every measurement you make has an uncertainty. Understanding that uncertainty is what separates a result from a guess.

Why It Matters

Headlines lie.
Errors don't.

A poll shows Harris at $49\% \pm 3\%$ and Trump at $46\% \pm 3\%$. The headline screams a "surge" — but the two numbers overlap completely within their uncertainties.

Without understanding error, you cannot tell signal from noise. This applies in politics, climate science, medicine — and every physics lab you will ever do.

Why It Matters

Is 2024 really the hottest year on record?

Global average temperature in 2024 was reported as $+1.60\,^\circ\text{C}$ above pre-industrial levels — about $0.12\,^\circ\text{C}$ warmer than the previous record set in 2023.

But the instrumental uncertainty in global temperature averages is roughly $\pm0.10\,^\circ\text{C}$. That $0.12\,^\circ\text{C}$ difference barely exceeds the measurement error.

The record is likely real — but quantifying uncertainty is precisely how science makes that claim responsibly.

Measuring Gravitational Acceleration

Why It Matters

Do these two measurements disagree?

Student A measures $g = 9.85 \pm 0.20 \ \text{m/s}^2$. Student B measures $g = 9.75 \pm 0.15 \ \text{m/s}^2$.

The central values differ by 0.10 m/s² — but their uncertainty ranges overlap substantially. Both are consistent with the accepted value $g = 9.81 \ \text{m/s}^2$.

Knowing the error is what lets you make that judgment.

Measurement Basics

Why don't
measurements repeat?

Measure the same pencil $10$ times with a ruler and you won't get $12.7$ cm every time — you get $12.5$, $12.8$, $12.6$, $12.9$, etc.

This scatter isn't "bad" — it's data. Error does not mean blunder (misreading the scale is a mistake, to be caught and discarded); it is the irreducible spread that remains after every precaution has been taken.

Random error: discrete ruler markings, varying eye position, varying pressure.

Systematic error: a bent ruler always reads high; a cold ruler contracts; one-sided parallax.

Accuracy vs. Precision

Two different
kinds of error

Accuracy tells you how close your measurements are to the true value. An accurate measurement is right on target.

Precision tells you how reproducible your measurements are. A precise measurement is tightly clustered.

You can be precise but inaccurate (a broken clock is precise but usually wrong). You can be accurate but imprecise (lucky guesses). The goal is both: tight clusters at the true value.

Distributions & Mean

Where is your data clustered?

Measure a pendulum's period $N=50$ times and plot the values as a histogram — they cluster around $2.018$ s in a bell-shaped (normal) distribution.

The population mean $\mu$ is the true period (unknown). The sample mean $\bar{x}$ is what you calculate from your $N$ measurements:

$\displaystyle \bar{x} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i$

The more measurements you take, the closer $\bar{x}$ gets to $\mu$. The bell shape is no accident: many small independent errors sum to an approximately Gaussian spread — the central limit theorem.

Standard Deviation

How clustered is your data?

The population standard deviation, $\sigma$, is how spread out the population is. $\sigma^2$ is called the population variance. The spread in your data reflects the spread in the population. The sample standard deviation $s$ estimates it:

$\displaystyle s^2 = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \bar{x})^2$

We divide by $N-1$, not $N$, because $\bar{x}$ itself was estimated from the data; dividing by $N$ would systematically underestimate the spread. For a Gaussian distribution, about 68% of measurements fall within $\bar{x} \pm s$. The more measurements you take, the closer $s$ gets to $\sigma$.

Poisson Statistics

Counting has its
own special rule

When you count discrete events (radioactive decays, photons, particles, spam emails), the results follow a Poisson distribution with a unique property:

$\Delta n = \sqrt{n}$

The statistical uncertainty in a count equals the square root of the count itself. Observe 100 decays and $\Delta n = 10$; observe 10,000 and $\Delta n = 100$. When the expected count is unknown, use the measured count as the estimate.

Of 1595 spam emails, 784 arrive by day and 811 by night — each count uncertain by $\sqrt{n} \approx 28$. The difference (27) is comparable to the uncertainty, so there is no statistically significant preference.

Error in the Mean

Combining Samples

Each data sample has its own mean $\bar{x}$ and standard deviation $s$. Repeat the whole experiment many times and the sample means themselves form a distribution — much narrower than the data.

The standard error of the mean (SEM) is:

$\displaystyle \sigma_{\bar{x}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{N}}$

Report a series of $N$ measurements as $\bar{x} \pm \sigma_{\bar{x}}$; there is a 68% probability that the true mean lies in this interval. Each time you double $N$, the error shrinks by $\sqrt{2}$. This is why experiments repeat measurements: averaging is powerful.

Error Propagation

How errors flow
through calculations

For a small uncertainty $\sigma_x$, the uncertainty in a computed result $f(x)$ is:

$\sigma_f \approx \left| \frac{df}{dx} \right| \sigma_x$

The derivative amplifies or dampens the error — area inherits twice the relative error of radius, since $A \propto r^2$.

Several independent measurements combine in quadrature:

$\displaystyle \Delta Z = \sqrt{ \left(\frac{\partial Z}{\partial A}\,\Delta A\right)^{2} + \left(\frac{\partial Z}{\partial B}\,\Delta B\right)^{2} }$

Pitfall: if the same quantity appears twice (e.g. $Z = A^2$), the inputs are not independent — differentiate directly: $\Delta Z = 2A\,\Delta A$.

Reporting Results

Round to
what you know

The last significant figure of a result should sit in the same decimal place as its uncertainty. Round the uncertainty to one or two significant figures first, then match the result to it.

Leading zeros are never significant; trailing zeros after a decimal point always are. Write $4 \times 10^2$ (or $400.$) when trailing zeros are ambiguous.

Adding or subtracting: keep the largest last significant place ($89.332 + 1.1 \to 90.4$). Multiplying or dividing: keep the fewest significant figures ($2.80 \times 4.5039 \to 12.6$).

Summary

You now have
the tools

This is your toolkit for understanding and quantifying measurement uncertainty.

Always ask: How big is my error? Does my measurement answer the question? Do my results agree with others?

Error analysis turns raw data into reliable conclusions. When you report a result, report its uncertainty too. That's how science works.
